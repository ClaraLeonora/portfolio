About

Studying in Hong Kong and traveling across mainland China last spring, I kept a running list of things I couldn't open: a paper I needed, a course platform, a search I'd run a hundred times at home. It was a small inconvenience for me, and a permanent condition for the students around me. That experience directly inspired my current research, where I lead a three-person team investigating whether learners who rely on privacy tools like Tor can tell when an educational platform leaks their data or quietly blocks them.

This thread, making sure technology actually works for the specific person using it, runs through all of my work. As a STARS AI Scholar, I designed AI and robotics activities for K–12 students with disabilities, where a child's participation came down to whether we had explicitly planned for them. In Tiffany Barnes's Game2Learn Lab, I evaluated five prompting techniques for LLM-generated logic proofs and introduced a bidirectional method that, to my surprise, lost to the simplest technique I tested (Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence, 2025). To address the usability gaps our lab's survey data revealed, where half of students cited the interface rather than the logic as their biggest hurdle, I designed and prototyped Proof-42, a mobile logic tutor scaffolded by cognitive-load principles, with screen-reader support and color-blind palettes built in from the start.

I hold a B.S. in Mathematics and am completing a second B.S. in Computer Science with a minor in Business, a perspective shaped by TAing introductory Java and engineering backend services for NASA's Deep Space Network at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory. I approach educational technology as a systems problem: one that requires mathematical precision, pedagogical intent, and accessible design.

Underneath all of it is one question: how do we audit and design learning systems so they don't fail restricted or marginalized users?
